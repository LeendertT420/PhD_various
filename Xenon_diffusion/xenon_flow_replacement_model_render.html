Modeling Xenon Delivery by Saturated-Liquid Replacement

A mass-transfer framework for comparison with a sealed headspace
4 November 2026
Abstract. This note formulates a xenon-delivery experiment in which liquid is equilibrated with xenon in an external saturator and then flows into a vessel containing an organoid. The vessel liquid is progressively replaced rather than pressurized by a sealed headspace. Two vessel descriptions are given: a well-mixed control volume and a spatial advection–dispersion model. Both can be coupled to radial xenon diffusion in a spherical organoid. Assumptions, boundary conditions, equilibrium predictions, and measurements needed for comparison are stated explicitly.

1. Physical picture and modeling distinction

The incoming and resident liquids are generally the same solvent; they are not two immiscible fluids with a sharp material interface. The quantity transported through the vessel is dissolved xenon. Bulk liquid motion carries xenon by advection, while molecular diffusion and flow-induced mixing smooth concentration gradients. A sharp replacement front may be a useful approximation at high Péclet number, but is not required.

The external saturator supplies a prescribed xenon concentration. Liquid replacement changes the vessel concentration, and xenon then diffuses into the organoid. Unlike a sealed-headspace experiment, the incoming liquid is an ongoing source: the vessel's final concentration is not found by conserving the initial sealed-vessel inventory.

2. Xenon concentration in the incoming liquid

Let Ps(t) be the xenon partial pressure in the saturator, T the absolute temperature, and Rg the gas constant. At gas–liquid equilibrium, Henry's law in concentration-over-pressure convention gives

Cin(t)=Hcp(T)Ps(t).

In the project's partition notation this may be written Cin=λlPs/(RgT), provided λl is defined consistently. Henry-constant conventions differ, so units and definition must accompany any numerical value [1], [2].

If saturation is incomplete, Henry's law is an equilibrium target rather than the actual outlet concentration. A lumped, well-mixed saturator can be approximated as

dCsdt=kLa(HcpPs−Cs),Cin=Cs.

Here Cs is liquid concentration in the saturator and kLa is a volumetric gas–liquid transfer coefficient. This is a reduced model, not a universal saturator equation; an actual contactor may need a plug-flow or staged gas–liquid model.

3. Vessel option A: well-mixed liquid

If mixing is fast compared with replacement and uptake, represent the vessel by one concentration Cv(t). For constant liquid volume Vl, equal inflow/outflow Q, and one spherical organoid of radius R, the xenon balance is

VldCvdt=Q(Cin−Cv)−AoJ(t),Ao=4πR2.

The flow term is xenon delivered minus xenon removed by outflow. J>0 is uptake from liquid into tissue, in mol m−2 s−1. Without uptake and with constant inlet concentration,

Cv(t)=Cin+[Cv(0)−Cin]e−t/τflow,τflow=Vl/Q.

Vl/Q is the ideal well-mixed replacement time; tissue uptake can delay vessel concentration from approaching the inlet value.

Tissue diffusion and liquid–tissue coupling

For a homogeneous spherical organoid with effective xenon diffusivity Dt, tissue concentration Ct(r,t) follows

∂Ct∂t=Dtr2∂∂r(r2∂Ct∂r),0<r<R.

Center symmetry imposes ∂rCt|r=0=0. With negligible liquid-film resistance, instantaneous surface partition gives

Ct(R,t)=λt/lCv(t),J(t)=Dt∂rCt|r=R.

This sign convention makes uptake positive when concentration is higher at the tissue surface than at its center. For finite liquid-film resistance, introduce surface concentration Cl,s and use

J=kl(Cv−Cl,s)=Dt∂rCt|r=R,Ct(R,t)=λt/lCl,s.

kl is the liquid-side mass-transfer coefficient. Further membrane or interfacial resistance can be added in series if needed.

4. Vessel option B: spatial advection–dispersion

When gradients along the vessel matter, a one-dimensional cross-section-averaged model is

∂Cl∂t+u∂Cl∂z=Dax∂2∂z2Cl.

u is mean velocity and Dax is effective axial dispersion, including molecular diffusion and unresolved spreading due to the velocity profile and mixing. Taylor's tube-flow analysis explains shear-enhanced effective spreading [3]. Danckwerts inlet and outlet conditions are

uCl(0,t)−Dax∂zCl|z=0=uCin(t),∂zCl|z=Lv=0.

An initial profile Cl(z,0) is also required. This model describes liquid xenon transport, not uptake by one isolated organoid. For one organoid, resolve its surrounding liquid and couple surface flux to the tissue equation. For a distributed population of organoids, a homogenized sink is possible:

∂Cl∂t+u∂Cl∂z=Dax∂2∂z2Cl−asJ.

as is organoid area per liquid volume. Do not spread one organoid's uptake uniformly along the vessel unless that is an intentional volume-averaged approximation.

5. Equilibrium and step changes

For a constant, fully equilibrated inlet maintained indefinitely, with no reaction or degradation, the final state is spatially uniform:

Cv→Cin,Ct(r)→λt/lCin.

This sustained-feed steady state differs from sealed-headspace equilibrium, where finite xenon inventory redistributes among gas, liquid, and tissue. For equal increments ΔPs, the ideal inlet level after k steps is

Cin,k=HcpkΔPs.

During finite intervals, liquid and tissue concentrations lag the equilibrium reference levels. Saturator dynamics or tubing dead volume can also delay pressure steps before they reach the vessel.

6. Dimensionless scales and comparison

τflow=Vl/Q,τtissue=R2/Dt,Pe=uLv/Dax,Fot=Dtt/R2.

Flow time estimates replacement in a mixed-volume approximation; tissue diffusion time estimates internal equilibration. The Péclet number compares axial advection with effective axial dispersion. Compare sealed and flow-through systems using the same organoid parameters, temperature, initial tissue state, and final inlet xenon level. Compare vessel/interface concentration histories and tissue-average or center-to-surface histories, not nominal pressures alone.

7. Recommended first implementation and measurements

Start with the well-mixed balance plus spherical tissue diffusion when vessel mixing is fast. Use the advection–dispersion equation when replacement fronts or axial gradients matter. Measure or specify:

  1. Q, Vl, vessel geometry, and initial liquid xenon level;
  2. saturator pressure, temperature, contact time, and preferably measured inlet xenon concentration;
  3. organoid radius, number, exposed area, and placement in flow;
  4. liquid xenon diffusivity, effective tissue diffusivity, and tissue/liquid partition ratio at experiment temperature;
  5. whether liquid-side or membrane transfer resistance is significant; and
  6. vessel concentration versus time, flow rate, and timing of saturator pressure changes.

Do not automatically substitute a water Henry constant for the project's fitted λl, or assume a universal tissue partition factor. Values depend on temperature, medium, tissue, and the precise coefficient convention [2], [5].
